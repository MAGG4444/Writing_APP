# Beat Sheet Pacing

Distilled from the user's writing craft library after reading the source text. This skill is original guidance for the writing agent; do not quote or reproduce the books.

## Use This Skill For

- Generating chapter outlines.
- Turning a project outline into slow, readable chapter progression.
- Checking whether a chapter jumps through too many locations or revelations.
- Drafting from an existing outline without rushing the main plot.

## Core Thesis

Before structure, clarify the promise: what is this story, who is it for, and why should a stranger care? Then use beats to keep that promise without rushing.

For novels, adapt screenplay beat logic as pacing pressure, not page-count law.

## Premise Gate

Before planning chapters, test the premise:

- **Clear hook**: Can it be explained in one or two sentences?
- **Irony or reversal**: Is there a built-in contradiction, uncomfortable fit, or pressure that creates immediate curiosity?
- **Emotional reach**: Does the premise imply a human longing, fear, humiliation, or desire?
- **Audience promise**: What reading appetite does it satisfy: mystery, romance, dread, wonder, revenge, healing, power fantasy, literary introspection?
- **Freshness inside familiarity**: What known story pleasure does it offer, and what makes this version different?

If this gate fails, do not compensate with more plot. Clarify the promise.

## Protagonist Gate

State the story as: "This is about a [specific kind of person] who wants [relatable goal] but is trapped by [specific pressure/opposition]."

The protagonist should:

- create strong conflict with the premise;
- have a goal the reader can understand quickly;
- be the person who suffers the most meaningful transformation;
- have at least one early behavior that earns curiosity, sympathy, admiration, or concern.

## Novel Beat Spine

Use these as long-form structure markers:

- **Opening image**: current world, tone, lack, wound, or false safety.
- **Theme pressure**: an early line, choice, or contrast that points to the story's real question.
- **Setup**: ordinary pattern, key relationships, missing thing, and fragile plan.
- **Catalyst**: disruption that cannot be ignored.
- **Debate**: hesitation, misread, denial, attempted escape.
- **Act-two doorway**: decision or forced crossing into a new situation.
- **Promise section**: deliver the genre pleasure and premise fun while complications grow.
- **Midpoint shift**: apparent win/loss, new information, changed stakes.
- **Pressure closes in**: outer opposition strengthens and inner weakness starts costing more.
- **Low point**: visible loss plus emotional interpretation.
- **Reframe**: protagonist understands the problem differently.
- **Final movement**: action based on the new understanding.
- **Closing image**: changed world or changed self.

For a slow novel, one marker may span many chapters.

## Chapter Beat Pattern

For slow-paced chapters, center one dramatic unit:

- one primary location or situation;
- one immediate objective;
- one resistance source;
- one small but irreversible change;
- one hook, question, or emotional aftertaste.

Use 3-5 beats:

1. **Entry pressure**: what the character is trying to do right now.
2. **Friction**: what blocks, delays, tempts, or complicates it.
3. **Discovery or shift**: one small new fact, feeling, risk, or relationship change.
4. **Choice or cost**: the character responds and pays something.
5. **Exit hook**: end with sharpened uncertainty, not a solved arc.

## Pacing Rules

- Do not stack too many major turns in one chapter.
- A chapter can be quiet if the reader's question becomes sharper.
- Side scenes should mirror, complicate, or relieve the main pressure, not wander away from it.
- "Bad guys close in" can mean social pressure, worsening evidence, inner temptation, time pressure, suspicion, shame, or contradiction, not only a villain.
- A subplot should carry theme, contrast, or emotional oxygen; it is not filler.
- Delay revelations by turning them into smaller partial discoveries.
- For slow fiction, prefer one scene becoming more charged over multiple scene jumps.

## Revision Diagnostics

If a chapter feels rushed:

- identify how many locations, revelations, confrontations, and decisions it contains;
- split any two major turns that both change the plot direction;
- move global revelations into local clues;
- replace summary with one concrete exchange, object, hesitation, or physical obstacle;
- end before the explanation finishes.

If a chapter feels empty:

- add an immediate objective;
- make refusal or delay cost something;
- give the scene one new external consequence and one internal consequence;
- make a later chapter depend on this chapter's change.

## Output Checklist

When using this skill, include:

- Target chapter number.
- Current structural marker, if known.
- Primary scene/situation.
- Character's immediate objective.
- Resistance.
- Single information increment.
- Emotional or relational shift.
- Ending hook.
- Explicit note about what not to reveal yet.
